Data preprocessing

  • Rebuild TEPI's 12 indicators from raw Census tables, with TEPI's own definitions
    • Found by testing alternatives: some divide household counts by total population; people of color excludes Hispanic white residents; no insurance counts the uninsured under 65 only
    • Check: 9 of 12 come from the 2019–23 release: 8 match TEPI exactly, poverty in 78% of block groups (r = 0.999)
  • Fill the 3 TEPI-only indicators (unemployment, children, seniors)
    • They match no ACS release (likely Esri estimates): TEPI's published values are used for every ACS release
    • 1990 and 2000 use that census's own values
  • Remap releases older than 2016–20 onto today's 2020 block groups
    • From 1990, 2000 and 2010 block groups by area: a spatial join of the old and new boundaries
    • Check: at the seam (2015–19 vs 2016–20) rates still correlate 0.80–0.90 across block groups
  • Handle what is missing or different
    • 1990 and 2000 have no insurance or disability question: TEPI is recomputed without them
    • Income in 2024 dollars (CPI-U); margins of error carried through for the ACS
  • Recompute TEPI for all 14 releases
    • Percentile ranks, averaged up TEPI's tree, cut into 5 tiers
    • Check: the 2019–23 recompute matches the published TEPI (ρ = 1.00, 98.5% same tier)

Output: one table, tepi_timeseries.csv (718 block groups × 14 releases), plus a network of neighbouring block groups for Moran's I.

How TEPI turns 12 census indicators into a priority tier

Hover a box to trace what feeds into it and what it feeds. Line width = that input's share of the final average. Box values (P) are for the what-if block group below.

What-if: set a block group's indicator values

Values are percentages of residents or households. Each one is ranked against the real block groups, exactly as TEPI does, and the diagram updates.

Construction steps

  1. Select indicators. 12 block-group percentages (Esri Business Analyst / ACS). Chosen from a review of existing vulnerability indices, limited to demographic variables, and revised by a community survey. Every one is read as "higher % = more vulnerable".
  2. Percentile-rank each indicator across all block groups:
    P = (rank − 1) / (n − 1) × 100
    Chosen to reduce outlier influence and keep rank, not magnitude. Ties share the lowest rank, so 0% gets P = 0.
  3. Sub-index = mean of its inputs' percentiles, then re-ranked. Five sub-indices; Age (children, seniors) is nested inside Physical.
  4. Theme = mean of its sub-index percentiles: Economic (3) and Personal (2), then re-ranked.
  5. TEPI = mean of the Economic and Personal percentiles, then re-ranked. The mean is compensatory: a high value on one input can offset a low one.
  6. Decision heuristic: the TEPI percentile is cut into quintiles, giving five tiers of about 143 block groups each. "High" priority = the top 20%.

Equal weights at each level do not give equal weights overall. Because the tree is uneven, low English proficiency and education each carry 12.5% of the final average, while children and seniors carry 4.2%.

Why differential privacy?

  • Published tables can be turned back into people
    • The Census Bureau rebuilt person records (block, sex, age, race, ethnicity) from 6.2 billion statistics in the 2010 Census tables
    • Exactly right for 46% of the population; linked to commercial data, about 52 million identities confirmed
  • The response: noise on purpose
    • Since the 2020 Census, every published count carries calibrated random noise (differential privacy, the TopDown algorithm)
    • The ACS does not use it today; the decennial census does
  • TEPI is exposed
    • Built from the smallest published geography, block groups
    • About small groups: the unemployed, households without a vehicle, often only 20–25 people in a block group
  • Our question: if TEPI's inputs were protected this way, would its answers survive?
    • Hot spots on the neighbour network (T3) and the similarity view (PCA, T1)

Source: M. Hawes, U.S. Census Bureau (2020), database reconstruction experiment on the 2010 Census.

How differential privacy works

  • The guarantee
    • Any output is almost as likely with or without any one person:
      P[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ eε · P[M(D′) ∈ S]
    • Smaller ε = stronger privacy = more noise; budgets add up over everything published
  • The Laplace mechanism
    • One person changes a count by at most 1
    • So add noise η ~ Laplace(0, 1/ε): typical size √2/ε people, whatever the count
    • Round back to whole counts, 0 ≤ numerator ≤ denominator
  • In our system
    • Noise on every numerator and denominator behind TEPI's 12 inputs (2019–23), TEPI recomputed, Moran's I and PCA re-run; 20 runs per ε
    • Per-count ε: far stricter than the Census, whose 2020 redistricting data split a total ε = 19.61 (17.14 persons + 2.47 housing units) over all tables and levels, with discrete Gaussian noise

Mistakes I caught

  • Time curves built per block group, not per group (I read the paper)
  • A map slider running backwards; district shapes not aligned between years
  • A Moran's I axis zoomed so tight that noise looked large
  • District hues in TEPI blue; tooltips off the screen; a slow hot-spot page
  • Unverified facts: a privacy budget quoted from memory; Laplace called the Census's mechanism

How I checked

  • Every number against something independent: published TEPI, the official canvass, the seam between block-group vintages
  • Every view by using it on a question: if I could not read the answer, it was redone
  • Claims sourced or cut: the ε of 19.61 checked against the Census Bureau's release

Lessons. The AI raised the floor, not the ceiling. It was fast and good at checking numbers, but it optimised what existed: it never proposed the questions, and its first visual design was usually cluttered or ambiguous. The design work, which view, which encoding, which interaction, stayed mine. What helped: naming the method or paper, asking it to prove claims against data, and a fresh agent for critique.
