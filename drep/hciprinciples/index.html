Two schools and a middle ground

Should people control software directly, or delegate work to software that acts for them? Shneiderman’s direct manipulation against Maes’s interface agents, and Horvitz’s mixed-initiative middle ground.

Direct manipulationYou do it yourself, by grabbing and moving things on screen. Like driving your own car.Shneiderman, 1983
Interface agentsYou hand the task to software that does it for you. Like telling a driver where to go.Maes, 1994
Mixed initiativeYou and the software take turns; it steps in only when that’s likely to help. Like a driving instructor with a second brake.Horvitz, 1999 · today’s reading

Before 1983, most people typed commands

Direct manipulation means working on the thing itself: you drag the file, move the slider or click the cell, and see the result right away.

To copy a file you had to remember a command, its spelling and its order of arguments. Shneiderman asked why some new systems felt so different.

Shneiderman · 1983

Three principles of direct manipulation

The principles

  1. Keep the objects visible. The document, cells or files stay on screen the whole time.
  2. Act, don’t type. Point, drag or press a labeled button instead of writing a command.
  3. Small, fast, undoable steps. Each action shows its effect at once and can be reversed.

What users gain

  • Novices learn quickly, often by watching someone else.
  • Experts work fast.
  • Occasional users remember how it works.
  • Error messages are rarely needed.
  • You see right away if you’re getting closer to your goal.
  • You feel in control.

      Where it runs out

      What direct manipulation struggles with

      • Scale. 718 block groups fit on a screen; fifty thousand emails don’t.
      • Repetition. There is nothing to drag for “do this every month.”
      • Hidden things. Future events and rules have no icon to grab.
      • Learning the visuals. A lasso or a UMAP plot still has to be explained.
      • Access. It assumes you can see and point.
      • Effort. You do every step yourself.

      After 1983

      Where direct manipulation went next

      The same idea, grabbing the thing itself, moved off the desktop: into physical objects, hardware and code.

      Tangible interfaces

      More ↗

      Ishii & Ullmer’s “Tangible Bits” (1997). In Urp (1999), planners moved model buildings on a table and saw projected shadows move. Drag the buildings and change the time.

      Physical computing

      More ↗

      Maes · 1994
      
  
      Too much to do by hand
      
  
      An interface agent is software that does a task for you, like an assistant, instead of you doing every step yourself.
      
  
      
    
      The problem
      
      
        
        
      • Information overload. More email, news and files than anyone can sort.
        • 
        
      • New users. Most people using computers were never trained.
        • 
        
      • Direct manipulation doesn’t scale. You still do every step yourself.
        • 
      
      
    
      Her proposal
      
      
        
        
      • Indirect management. Alan Kay’s phrase: you delegate, the computer acts.
        • 
        
      • Interface agent. A personal assistant that works beside you in the same program.
        • 
        
      • Delegation. Handing over a task, and with it some control.
        • 
      
      
  
      
  
      Maes, P. (1994). Agents that reduce work and information overload. Communications of the ACM 37(7).
      

      


      
  
      Maes · 1994
      
  
      Every agent has two problems
      
  
      
    
      The two problems
      
      
        
        
      • Competence. How does it know when to help, and how?
        • 
        
      • Trust. Will you feel safe letting it act for you?
        • 
      
      
    
      Three ways to build one
      
      
      
        
      ApproachCompetenceTrust
      
        
          
      You write the rulesOnly as good as your rules; you must spot every pattern and keep the rules up to date.High: you wrote them.
      
          
      Experts build it inLots of work up front; fixed, and not fitted to you.Low: you don’t know what it knows.
      
          
      It learns from you (Maes)Grows as it watches you work.Grows as you watch it get things right.
      
              
      
      
  
      

      


      
  
      Maes · 1994
      
  
      How her agents learn
      
  
      
    
      Four sources
      
      
        
        
      1. Watching you. It records what you do in each situation and imitates it.
        2. 
        
      2. Your feedback. It learns when you accept or undo what it did.
        3. 
        
      3. Examples. You can show it a case on purpose.
        4. 
        
      4. Other agents. It can ask agents that serve other people.
        5. 
      
      
    
      When it acts
      
      
        
        
      • Confidence. It compares the new case with similar past ones.
        • 
        
      • “Tell me” threshold. Above it, the agent suggests.
        • 
        
      • “Do it” threshold. Above it, the agent acts on its own.
        • 
        
      • You set both. You decide how much to hand over.
        • 
      
      
  
      
  
      Works best when tasks repeat a lot and differ from person to person.

          Where it runs out
          
  
          What interface agents struggle with
          
  
            
    
          • Slow start. It is no help until it has watched you for a while, as in the email demo.
            • 
    
          • New situations. A sender or task it has never seen leaves it unsure, like the email from Dad.
            • 
    
          • Silent mistakes. When it acts on its own, errors can go unnoticed, like the “URGENT” email filed away.
            • 
    
          • Hidden choices. It decides things you didn’t ask about, like what “similar” means in the TEPI agent.
            • 
    
          • Only repeated tasks. It needs a pattern to learn; one-off tasks give it nothing.
            • 
    
          • Privacy. To learn your habits, it has to watch everything you do.
            • 
  
          

          


          
  
          After 1994
          
  
          Where interface agents went next
          
  
          
    
          Learning from you
          
      
            
        
          • Recommendations. Ringo became Firefly (1996); Amazon, Netflix and Spotify use the same “people like you” idea.
            • 
        
          • Spam filters (2000s). Every “mark as spam” teaches the filter.
            • 
        
          • Priority inboxes (2010s). Mail apps learn which messages you open first, like Maxims.
            • 
      
          
    
          Agents with a face, and a warning
          
      
            
        
          • Microsoft’s Office Assistant, “Clippy” (1997). Popped up with help nobody asked for, and became a famous failure.
            • 
        
          • Its ideas came from the Lumière project at Microsoft Research, led by Eric Horvitz. Hold on to that name.
            • 
        
          • Voice assistants (2010s). Siri and Alexa act on spoken requests.
            • 
      
          
  
          

          


          
  
          IUI ’97 and CHI ’97
          
  
          The debate: Shneiderman vs. Maes
          
  
          
    
          Shneiderman: keep people in charge
          
      
            
        
          • Control. People want to feel they did it, not that something did it for them.
            • 
        
          • Predictability. An agent that changes as it learns is hard to anticipate.
            • 
        
          • Responsibility. When an agent errs, who is accountable?
            • 
        
          • Fake people fail. Talking, human-like interfaces such as Microsoft Bob flopped.
            • 
      
          
    
          Maes: people need help
          
      
            
        
          • Overload. There is too much information to handle by hand.
            • 
        
          • Not a replacement. Agents sit beside direct manipulation; you can still do it yourself.
            • 
        
          • Not a person. An agent doesn’t need a face or a human personality.
            • 
        
          • Trust is earned. It learns from you, explains itself, and you set how much it may do.
            • 
      
          
  
          
  
          Shneiderman, B. & Maes, P. (1997). Direct manipulation vs. interface agents. Interactions 4(6).

          Horvitz · 1999 · today’s reading
          
  
          Stop choosing sides
          
  
          Mixed initiative means you and the computer share the work. Either one can take the lead, and the computer steps in only when that is likely to help.
          
  
          
    
          The idea
          
      
            
        
          • Keep direct manipulation. You can always do it yourself.
            • 
        
          • Add automated help. The computer offers to do parts of the task.
            • 
        
          • Decide when to help. Weigh how sure it is against what a wrong guess would cost you.
            • 
      
          
    
          His example: Lookout
          
      
            
        
          • An add-on for Outlook email. Built at Microsoft Research.
            • 
        
          • It reads incoming mail. It guesses whether you want to put a meeting on your calendar.
            • 
        
          • Then it does nothing, asks, or acts, depending on how sure it is.
            • 
      
          
  
          
  
          Horvitz, E. (1999). Principles of mixed-initiative user interfaces. Proceedings of CHI ’99, 159–166.
          

          


          
  
          Horvitz · 1999
          
  
          What “utility” means
          
  
          Utility is a score for how good or bad an outcome is for you. Positive helps you; negative costs you. It’s like deciding whether to carry an umbrella: how likely is rain, and how bad is getting wet compared with carrying it for nothing?
          
  
          
    
          Lookout’s four outcomes
          
      
          
        
          You wanted a meetingYou didn’t
          
        
          
          It adds the event+6
          Saved you the work          		−5
          You have to delete it
          
          
          It stays quiet0
          You do it yourself          		0
          Nothing happens
          
                  
      
          
      
          The numbers are made up for this example; Horvitz’s point is that a system should weigh them.
          
    
          Expected utility: weigh each outcome by its chance
          
      
          
      
          
    
          
  
          

          


          
  
          Horvitz · 1999
          
  
          Decision theory, in one picture
          
  
          Decision theory is a way to choose when you can’t be sure what will happen: list your options, how likely each outcome is, and how good each outcome would be, then pick the option with the highest expected utility.
          
  
          
    
          The four ingredients, for Lookout
          
      
            
        
          • Actions: stay quiet, ask, or add the event.
            • 
        
          • What might be true: you want a meeting, or you don’t.
            • 
        
          • Probability: how sure it is, from reading the email.
            • 
        
          • Utility: how good each outcome is for you.
            • 
      
          
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
          
  
          

          


          
  
          Horvitz · 1999
          
  
          Act, ask, or stay quiet?
          
  
          
    
          
      
          Same idea with a third option, asking. Each choice has a payoff (its utility):
          
      
            
        
          • Stay quiet: nothing gained, nothing lost.
            • 
        
          • Act: a big win if you wanted it, annoying if you didn’t.
            • 
        
          • Ask: a smaller win, at the small cost of interrupting you.
            • 
      
          
      
          
        
          
        
          
        
          
        
          
      
          
    
          
    
          
      
      
          
    
          
  
          
  
          Horvitz calls this choosing the action with the highest expected utility: the payoff of each choice, weighted by how likely you are to want help. The two crossing points are his thresholds.
          

          


          
  
          Horvitz · 1999
          
  
          Twelve principles, in plain words
          
  
          
    
          Be worth it
            
      
          1. Add real value beyond what you could easily do yourself.
            2. 
      
          2. Keep learning by watching what you do.
          
    
          Know what you don’t know
            
      
          1. Be unsure about goals; don’t assume.
            2. 
      
          2. Weigh costs and benefits before acting.
            3. 
      
          3. Ask when a question would settle it.
            4. 
      
          4. Help less precisely when unsure, e.g. open the right week, not the exact hour.
          
    
          Respect the person
            
      
          1. Mind attention: don’t interrupt at a bad moment.
            2. 
      
          2. Easy to call up and to dismiss.
            3. 
      
          3. Make bad guesses cheap to ignore or undo.
            4. 
      
          4. Behave politely, as people expect.
          
    
          Work together
            
      
          1. Let the person refine what the agent produced.
            2. 
      
          2. Remember recent interaction so you don’t repeat yourself.
          
  
          
  
          Numbers follow the paper’s list of “critical factors.”
