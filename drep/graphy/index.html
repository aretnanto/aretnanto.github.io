Summary · §1 Introduction

Problem and motivation

  • Charts are visual; blind and low-vision (BLV) readers rely on workarounds
    • Text descriptions: one fixed narration, no exploration
    • Sonification: good for trends, weak for exact values
    • Screen readers: values one at a time, no spatial overview
    • Static tactile graphics: spatial, but a new print per chart, not interactive
  • None supports interactive, multimodal, independent exploration
  • Opportunity: refreshable tactile display (RTD) + LLM agent
    • Touch for spatial understanding
    • Speech for calculation and analysis
Speaker notes

(≈45 s) Intro of the paper. The point: each existing approach covers part of the problem; none gives interactive, multimodal, independent exploration. RTD = pins raised and lowered dynamically. The bet: pair an RTD with a conversational LLM agent.

Starting point and contributions

  • Contributions
    • Graphy: RTD + LLM agent (ChatGPT with LangChain, pandas code over the data)
    • Design knowledge from four workshops over eight months

Summary · §2 Related work

Related work: what exists, what is missing

  • §2.1 Accessible data visualization
    • Printed tactile graphics: spatial but static, slow and costly to update
    • Descriptions, sonification, screen-reader navigation: none support interactive tactile exploration
  • §2.2 Refreshable tactile displays
    • Mostly used for art, illustrations and maps; little work on data visualization
    • Prior RTD studies suggested combining conversational speech with tactile representation
  • §2.3 Conversational agents
    • LLMs can answer questions about charts; BLV users develop strategies to verify LLM answers
  • Gap: no system tightly integrates touch, speech and tactile feedback for interactive data exploration
Speaker notes

(≈45 s) Walk through the three subsections briefly. §2.1: printed tactile graphics, descriptions, sonification, screen readers. §2.2: RTDs render new graphics in seconds; Dot Pad, Monarch, Graphiti; research mostly on art, illustrations and maps; Chundury et al. compared an RTD with audio-only and audio-tactile conditions and recommended combining conversational speech with tactile representation. §2.3: VizAbility, Kim et al. on BLV queries, Choe et al. (data-literate sighted users used the LLM as a specialist), Seo et al. (MAIDR plus an LLM: BLV users developed strategies to verify responses). The gap is the paper's own claim: no system tightly integrates touch, speech and tactile feedback.

Summary · §3 Graphy

Graphy: a conversational tactile data interface (CTDI)

  • Integrates touch, speech and tactile feedback; a multi-line RTD plus an LLM-powered agent
    • Line charts (single and multi-series), bar and stacked bar charts, scatterplots
  • Scaffolded chart exploration: interactive layers, each narrated and rendered on the RTD; skip or revisit at your own pace
  • Touch-driven data inspection: double-tap gestures or button stepping; each selection gives an audio label, a Braille label and a tactile highlight
  • Conversational data analysis: trends, calculations, comparisons; deictic queries like “what is the difference between these two?”
    • Responses split into one-sentence chunks, each with an animated highlight
  • Filtering for focus: isolate one series by voice
Speaker notes

(≈50 s) This is Section 3, the system the co-design produced. The five characteristics are the paper's own list. Emphasize that touch is the central activity; speech and the agent are layered on top. The filtering was mediated by a researcher in WS4, which the scenario slide shows.

Summary · §3 Graphy · implementation

How it is built

  • Display: Dot Pad RTD over USB serial (the most affordable multi-line RTD)
  • Touch: Ultraleap Leap Motion Controller mounted overhead tracks both index fingers
  • Speech: Picovoice wake word or push-to-talk; Google Cloud speech-to-text and text-to-speech
  • Charts: a Vega-Lite-to-RTD renderer
  • Agent: ChatGPT with LangChain fuses touch context with the query and runs pandas code over the chart’s data
    • Values are computed programmatically, not generated by the LLM
  • Open-source code is available (GitHub: accessible-data-vis/feelogue)
Speaker notes

(≈15 s) Quick. The point worth saying: the agent writes and runs pandas code, so numbers come from computation, not from the LLM text generation. The paper still notes LLMs can mishear or hallucinate, which is why the verify step matters. The Dot Pad has no built-in touch sensing, hence the external finger tracker.

Summary · §4 Co-design method

Method: parallel co-design

  • Three co-designers, all congenitally blind
    • D1 (24, legally blind), D2 (27, totally blind), D3 (55, totally blind)
    • Tactile-graphics experience: substantial (D1, D2), some (D3); all had used RTDs
  • Twelve one-on-one sessions: four workshops each, over eight months
  • Think-aloud method throughout: co-designers verbally described intended interactions, expectations and reasoning
  • Rounds
    • WS1: v1 prototype, single-series line chart
    • WS2: bar charts, layered overview, highlights
    • WS3: multi-series, stacked bars, scatterplot
    • WS4: free-form use on own data (avg 32 min), NASA-TLX, reflection
  • Aim: design knowledge, not a performance evaluation
Speaker notes

(≈40 s) Gaps between rounds: 2 months (WS1-WS2), 3 months (WS2-WS3), 1 week (WS3-WS4). Datasets were real: rainfall, interest rates, quarterly sales, vehicle efficiency. Paid $50 AUD/hour. Flag now that the authors say it is design knowledge, not a performance evaluation; return to this in the critique.

Summary · §5.3 Navigation, selection & interaction

Gestures to select, buttons to step, filtering to focus

  • Gesture selection: double-tap, familiar from mobile screen readers
    • Valued more as the finger tracker improved; D1 called it the most important feature by WS3
    • Audio, Braille and a tactile highlight fire together
  • Sequential interaction: explore by touch → select → confirm → then ask
  • Stepping: buttons move to the next value without having to find it (D1, suggested in WS2)
    • D2: precision and recovery when a gesture overshoots; D3: “voice-guided exploration”
    • Order is task-dependent: same segment across bars, or all segments within a bar?
  • Filtering: isolate a series to cut tactile clutter; D1: critical for complex charts
Speaker notes

(≈40 s) Section 5.3. Touch is sequential and local: reaching a data point means first locating it. Gestures suit known points; stepping suits unknown ones. Stepping order is task-dependent in multi-series and stacked bar charts, and in scatterplots you must fix one dimension and traverse the other.

Summary · §5.4 Tactile feedback

A tactile feedback grammar

  • v1 blinked one pin: too subtle, and pins can’t actuate under a finger
  • Static bounding box worked, but user and agent highlights looked identical
  • The grammar: who caused this highlight?
    • Gesture selection → static box
    • Button stepping → animates, then settles to static
    • Agent reference → animated box
  • Animating the data symbols instead was harder to interpret
  • Highlights persist until dismissed; one per finger
Speaker notes

(≈45 s) D1 refined the grammar; all three co-designers arrived at the same solution independently. D2's crosshair idea ('X marks the spot') was too large and looked like the cross symbol. D3 proposed an isolation mode that lowers nearby elements; not implemented.

Summary · §5.5 Agent interaction

The agent’s role

  • Used for what touch can’t resolve
    • Calculations and comparisons
    • Trend analysis
    • Visualization literacy (“how does a scatterplot work?”)
    • Operations (“filter everything”)
  • Responses
    • Answer first and short
    • But restate the query so mishearing can be caught
    • Consistent structure; chunked into sentences
  • Touch: spatial understanding. Agent: analytical depth
Speaker notes

(≈40 s) Push-to-talk replaced the unreliable wake word ('walkie-talkie with a robot'): deterministic control outweighed lifting a hand off the chart. D2 wanted the agent to explain what it can do.

Summary · §5.6.1 Observed interaction patterns (Fig. 8)

Select → confirm → ask → verify

Select→Confirm→Ask→Verify
  • Select by gesture or button; confirm by audio, Braille or tactile highlight
  • Sequential, unlike free touch while speaking in the earlier study
    • A real agent can misunderstand: confirm the point first
  • Ask the agent, which should acknowledge the touch context
  • Verify by tracing the chart
    • Distrust of LLMs; without the chart there is no way to check
Speaker notes

(≈35 s) Fig 8 in the paper. The design-pattern headline. Contrast with the Wizard-of-Oz study, where participants touched points while speaking; infallibility hid the risk. The fourth step, verify, emerged in free-form use.

Summary · §5.6.3 Retrospective reflections

Touch as agency and verification

  • Verification
    • D1: without the tactile display, “I have to trust the agent”
    • D2: “I don’t immediately trust anything with an LLM”; wants to see the actual data
    • D3: AI has been inaccurate before, so they verify it
  • Sensemaking
    • Touch gives a richer, better overall understanding than a verbal response (D1, D2), even if exact values are less clear
  • Autonomy and independence
    • D3: asking the agent is “the last resort”; D1: touch lets them consume data at their own pace
  • Strongest claim: they would still explore by touch even if the agent’s answers were guaranteed correct (e.g. computed from a spreadsheet)
Speaker notes

(≈60 s) Section 5.6.3, retrospective reflections. This is one of the most important parts of the paper. D1 described doing all three at once and said that without the tactile display, verification would not be possible. D2: verifying the agent's accuracy and building their own understanding through touch; distrusts anything LLM-based and wants to look at the actual data. D3: prior experience with AI giving inaccurate information; the agent is the last resort because they like to figure things out themselves. Sensemaking: D2 said the richness of information through touch is greater than a verbal response; D1 said touch gives a better overall understanding than a verbal summary even if exact details are less clear. Autonomy: D1 said touch lets them consume data at their own pace. The last bullet is my framing of these three motivations as agency plus verification.

Summary · §6.2 Layered presentation & responses

Why layered presentation matters

  • Sighted readers survey a chart instantly; BLV readers build it up feature by feature, and low resolution makes that harder
    • A single continuous narration (like alt text) was too long: audio and touch competed for attention
  • The co-designers’ solution: a layered overview, one component at a time, each with a spoken description
    • Each layer in isolation couples what you hear with what you feel
    • Charts stay interactive, and layers can be skipped or revisited
  • It grew from an orientation aid into the primary framework for exploring charts (WS4); D2 went from “gimmicky” to essential
  • Related: Olli does this for screen readers; tactile data comics are strictly presentational; layering adds interactive exploration
    • Same principle for agent answers: one-sentence chunks synced with animated highlights
Speaker notes

(≈45 s) Section 6.2. Core claim: layered presentation and response segmentation both structure multimodal output so touch and speech reinforce one another rather than compete. D3 suggested it could generalize beyond charts to other tactile graphics, supported by the tactile data comics work. In the scatterplot, all three co-designers traversed back and forth through the layers to build a conceptual understanding. Experienced users can skip layers: D2 skipped the interpretive summary but still wanted the structural content.

Summary · §6.3 Beyond the wizard

What a working system revealed that the Wizard-of-Oz study hid

  • When trust must be earned: a real agent can mishear or hallucinate → select, confirm, ask, then verify by touch
  • When there is no guide: a single narration was hard to follow → layered presentation
  • When data must be traversed: gesturing needs the point’s location → button stepping
  • When encoding must be rethought: the Wizard’s Graphiti had four pin heights; the Dot Pad is binary → symbols, clearance gaps, textures
    • Binary-pin constraint shared by most RTDs on the market
  • Longitudinal: four workshops over eight months, vs single 90–120 minute WOz sessions
Speaker notes

(≈40 s) Section 6.3 compares directly to the authors' earlier WOz study. Co-designers with tactile experience showed the same touch-first pattern as WOz participants. Also from §6.1: touch was the primary sensemaking channel; the agent was a data question-answering specialist; and layered presentation plus response segmentation share one purpose: structuring multimodal output so touch and speech reinforce rather than compete.

Summary · §6.4 Design recommendations (1/2)

Design recommendations for CTDIs (1/2)

  • Scaffold through layered presentation
    • Introduce chart components one at a time, each in isolation with a spoken description, to reduce clutter
    • Segment agent responses into navigable chunks with attention-drawing tactile feedback
    • Keep charts interactive so users can select values and query the agent
  • Fuse touch and speech in agent interaction
    • Touch gives spatial grounding; the agent gives calculation and analysis
    • Acknowledge the touch context before answering; keep responses concise and answer-first
  • Ground each interaction step before the next
    • Select (gesture or button) → confirm (audio, Braille, tactile) → query → verify by touch
Speaker notes

(≈40 s) The first recommendation is the layered presentation; spend a little longer here since the layered presentation is a key idea in the paper. Second: deictic queries should be acknowledged so people can confirm they were understood. Third: the select, confirm, ask, verify pattern from Fig 8.

Summary · §6.4 Design recommendations (2/2)

Design recommendations for CTDIs (2/2)

  • Provide complementary navigation mechanisms
    • Gestures for selecting known points; button stepping for traversing unknown points; filtering to reduce tactile clutter
  • Encode state with a tactile feedback grammar
    • Static highlights for gesture selections; animated highlights for agent references; transitional animations for stepping that settle to static
    • Highlights persist until dismissed; keep the grammar consistent across chart types
  • Design tactile encodings for RTD constraints
    • Visual conventions don’t transfer to touch, and print conventions don’t necessarily transfer to RTDs
    • Design for touch discriminability on pin arrays, and make encodings user-configurable, like screen-reader speech rate
Speaker notes

(≈40 s) Navigation: different goals need different mechanisms. Feedback grammar: user-initiated vs agent-initiated. Encodings: designed for the RTD's low resolution and binary pins, and configurable, since co-designers wanted adjustable gaps and symbols.

Limitations · §6.6 and my caveat

Limitations, and a caveat on my reading

  • Stated by the authors
    • Three congenitally blind co-designers with tactile experience; may not generalize to novices, late-blind or low-vision readers
    • Design knowledge, not a performance evaluation; agent accuracy not quantified
    • Binary-pin, braille-pitch display: some encodings may not transfer to future devices
    • Few, low-density chart types; scatterplots least resolved
    • Zooming and panning without losing spatial context remains open
  • Also visible in the paper
    • Filtering was mediated by a researcher in the WS4 scenario
    • The co-designers who shaped the system also rated it in WS4 (6–7 out of 7), though the authors call NASA-TLX a reflective prompt, not an evaluation
      • Co-design is necessary: designing without them would be a limitation too
      • But their ratings can’t speak for other readers; the paper does not say why no one else evaluated it
    • Needs an external finger tracker, a host computer and cloud services
  • Caveat on my reading: I am inexperienced in evaluating accessibility claims
    • I will review this as a general vis paper; other communities (accessibility, HCI, co-design) may weigh sample size, scale and evidence differently

My critique · engineering and privacy

What the interface leaves out

  • Engineering: height is unused
    • The Dot Pad is binary; the earlier Wizard-of-Oz study used the four-height Graphiti
    • The authors name multi-height as a hardware wish (§6.5), so series are told apart by symbols and textures, not elevation
  • Engineering: no haptics beyond raised pins
    • No vibration, force or texture feedback; highlights are pin animations that can’t actuate under a finger
    • I found no discussion of other haptic channels in the paper
  • Privacy: touch is private, voice is not
    • A screen can be angled away; touch needs proximity, so it is private by default
    • Spoken queries and answers can be heard by anyone nearby: the reader loses control of who learns about their data
    • I found no mention of privacy, headphones or shared spaces in the paper
Speaker notes

About 2 minutes. My own critique. Height: the authors acknowledge it in section 6.5 (Graphiti supports four heights but costs about $25,000), so be clear that this part is partly their own limitation; my addition is that other haptic channels (vibration, force, texture) are not even discussed. In my Pin Studio tool I added three pin-height levels as an extension, which you could mention. Privacy is entirely my observation: I searched the paper text for privacy, private, headphone, earphone, bystander and found nothing. The point ties back to agency: the interface is private through touch but public through voice, which is a trade the user may not control. Possible fix: headphones or bone conduction, but then environmental awareness is lost.

Discussion · about 6 minutes

Questions for the group

  • Embodiment: accessibility is framed as a tack-on to an interface treated as the ubiquitous default. Should the ubiquity itself be challenged?
    • Why is touch + audio an accommodation, not a general way to read data?
    • Who decides which interfaces get built, and for whom? What would a fairer representation look like?
  • Scale and sample size: three co-designers, a 60 × 40 display, low-density charts. Should visualization research only be done once it can scale?
    • Or are these limits the reason the work is needed?
  • Evidence: if not a large-n study, what would make you trust these recommendations?
  • Trust: readers verify the LLM by touch. Is a probabilistic agent the right tool here?
  • Physicalization: which of these ideas survive in a static printed plate like the one you held?
Speaker notes

Six minutes, so pick two or three questions; lead with embodiment. Embodiment (my argument): accessibility is treated as a tack-on to a default that is assumed to be ubiquitous. The paper tests touch and speech only with BLV co-designers, and no sighted reader touches the chart; the chart forms (line, bar, stacked bar, scatterplot) are inherited from visual convention, even though the authors note visual encoding standards don't transfer to touch. A vis venue (this is an IEEE VIS 2026 paper, published in TVCG) is exactly where the default-reader assumption should be challenged. Fair counterpoint to have ready: the co-design did give BLV co-designers a real say (layering, the feedback grammar and push-to-talk came from them, and touch-first was their own choice, for reasons of independence, verification and sensemaking), and the paper's goal was accessible visualization for BLV readers, not a general interface. Co-designers are necessary: designing without them would be a limitation too. The open issue is who else gets a say in the default, and who evaluates it beyond the people who built it. Scale and sample size: the usual critiques (n=3, 60x40 binary pins, low-density charts, no larger evaluation) are real, but they also describe why the work is needed: a small, hard-to-recruit population, hardware that is still low resolution, and no existing design guidance. Push on what counts as sufficient evidence for a design paper, and who gets left out if only scalable studies count. Physicalization links to the printed plates: ask people what they felt. Take Q&A on the paper first.
