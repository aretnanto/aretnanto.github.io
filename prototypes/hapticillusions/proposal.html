CSC 596 Visual Analytics · project proposal

Feel Data on Screen

A Pseudo-Haptic Prototyping Tool for Vis

Aditya Retnanto · November 4, 2026

Notes

Pseudo-haptics creates an illusion of touch, such as friction, with an ordinary mouse and screen. I want to use it to prototype felt encodings for visualization before anyone builds a device or prints an object. Open with a show of hands: who has felt a phone buzz or a game controller rumble? That is haptic feedback.

1 · Introduction

Visual analytics runs on vision

Visualization is “the use of computer-supported, interactive, visual representations of data to amplify cognition.”
Card, Mackinlay & Shneiderman 1999

  • Direct manipulation borrows the metaphor of handling an object, but the hand only moves a mouse (Shneiderman 1983).
  • The mouse is one-way. It tells the computer where the hand went and tells the hand nothing about the data.
  • Haptic feedback is touch output from a computer, like a phone buzzing. A desktop visualization has none.
Notes

The definition keeps the word visual. Input comes from the hand, but output only goes to the eyes. Direct manipulation borrows the language of touch, and the peripheral is what keeps touch out.

1 · Introduction

Real touch for data needs hardware or time

  • Data physicalization: data made into an object you can hold.
  • Novel engineered hardware: purpose-built devices that push, vibrate or move under the hand.
  • Few designers have either; all have a mouse.

Can we change what the hand perceives without changing what it senses? That depends on how far the brain trusts vision.

Jansen et al. 2015 · Follmer et al. 2013 · Le Goc et al. 2016, 2019 · Bae et al. 2022, 2023 · Lécuyer 2009

Notes

Novel engineered hardware renders in real time: force-feedback arms push back on a pen, shape displays raise motorized pins (inFORM), swarm robots move small wheeled robots into a chart (Zooids). Left chart: this hardware is fast, so speed is not its problem. Right chart: it has to be built in a lab. Prints need only a printer but take hours per change, and their touch is passive. Nothing is both fast and hardware-free. Read the two definitions aloud; most of the room will not have met these terms. Do not name pseudo-haptics yet: reveal the question and let the perception section answer it.

2 · Background · perception

How trustworthy is vision?

Psychology separates sensation, the signal a receptor picks up, from perception, what we experience.

Müller-Lyer

The two lines are the same length.

Ebbinghaus

The two orange circles are the same size.

Simultaneous contrast

The two squares are the same grey.

  • The image on the retina is ambiguous, so the brain combines the signal with a prior about what is likely (Helmholtz 1867, Knill & Pouget 2004).
  • Contrast comes from the wiring of the eye. The size illusions come from expectation, a rule that is usually right (Gregory 1968).

Goldstein & Brockmole 2016 · Müller-Lyer 1889 · Ebbinghaus · Helmholtz 1867 · Gregory 1968 · Knill & Pouget 2004

Notes

2 · Background · multisensory integration

When senses conflict, vision dominates

  • The brain weights each sense by how reliable it is (Ernst & Banks 2002).
  • On a screen, vision is the sharper cue, so the percept lands near what you see.
  • When vision is degraded, touch takes over: vision dominates because it is more reliable.

Ernst & Banks 2002 · McGurk & MacDonald 1976 · Alais & Burr 2004 · Botvinick & Cohen 1998 · Rock & Victor 1964

Notes

Same Bayesian weighting as the illusions, now across senses. The orange curve is what you perceive: close to vision, and sharper than either sense alone. The third bullet matters for Q&A: this only holds while vision is the more reliable cue, which it is on a screen.

2 · Background · pseudo-haptics

Can we design a multisensory illusion?

If vision wins on a screen, the visual signal can be designed on purpose to change what the hand perceives.

Pseudo-haptics

  • Change how far the cursor moves for the same hand movement, like turning mouse sensitivity down only over certain places. This is the control/display ratio.
  • Slowing an object on screen made people feel friction or resistance through a passive input device, with no force feedback (Lécuyer et al. 2000).
  • With only a mouse, people felt bumps and holes on a flat image (Lécuyer et al. 2004).

Lécuyer et al. 2000 · Lécuyer et al. 2004 · Lécuyer 2009 · Try it: aretnanto.github.io/prototypes/hapticillusions

Notes

Pause on the question, then press Next to reveal the answer. So far illusions were things that happen to us. Here one becomes a design material. Say “an illusion of friction”, not “touch”: there is no tactile stimulation at all.

2 · Background · pseudo-haptics

Changing perception, not sensation

  • Instant, with no extra hardware
  • Already on every desk, so a felt encoding can ship in any web page
  • Gives up realism: the touch is faked by vision
  • For trying encodings, not for replacing prints or engineered hardware
Notes

Callback to slide 3: same charts, the question mark is now answered. Fast, free and low fidelity is exactly what the proposed prototyping tool relies on. Next: how this sits against related work. Ubiquity is the second payoff: prints reach one person per object and devices one lab at a time, but a web page reaches anyone with a mouse or trackpad. It cuts both ways: the hardware is everywhere but not uniform. Mice, trackpads and pointer acceleration differ, so the tool asks the browser for raw, unaccelerated movement where it can.

3 · Technical approach

One engine behind every prototype

  • Computation: values are scaled to 0–1 and mapped to a gain, so the cursor slows over high values but never stops. Small peaks can be flattened first (topological persistence).
  • Visualization design: the picture and the felt layer read the same values, so they can be compared, or the picture hidden to test feel alone.
  • Interaction: the system pointer cannot be slowed, so the tool locks it and draws its own cursor. Every step is logged for replay.

Lécuyer et al. 2004 · Edelsbrunner, Letscher & Zomorodian 2002

Notes

The next slide gives the gain formula and its parameters. Map each card to a task: computation supports separating structure from noise, the shared visual layer supports finding a pattern by feel, and provenance supports reshaping and reviewing. The prototypes from the demo are this one engine with different data.

3 · Technical approach · parameters

How the cursor slows down

Δc = g(v) · Δh

  • v is the data value under the cursor, scaled from 0 to 1.
  • Δh is how far the hand moves the mouse; Δc is how far the cursor moves.
  • g is the gain. At 1 the cursor moves normally; below 1 it slows down.
  • The gain is g(v) = 1 − k · [qn(v)]γ, with three parameters: k, γ and n.
Notes

c is the drawn cursor, h the movement reported by the mouse, v the value under the cursor. The next slide shows what each parameter does.

3 · Technical approach · parameters

What k, γ and n change

  • k, strength. Higher k slows high values more. At 0.85 they move at 15% speed.
  • γ, where it slows. Below 1, low values already feel sticky; above 1, only high values do.
  • n, smooth or stepped. Steps give sharp borders; ∞ gives a gradual change.
  • τ, terrain only. Peaks shorter than τ are flattened first, so only significant peaks are felt.
  • Prototypes use k = 0.85, γ = 1, n = 4 for the heatmap and ∞ for terrain, and τ = 12% of the height range.
Notes

0.85 was chosen informally: strong enough to notice, and below 1 so the cursor never stops. Gamma shapes the curve without changing its end points. The heatmap rounds values to four levels, which is n = 4. These become designer controls in the tool.

3 · Technical approach · parameters

What the settings do to a sweep

  • Each dot is the cursor after one equal hand movement. Dots bunch where it slows.
  • The slowdown ratio is R = 1/(1 − k), so k = 0.85 gives R ≈ 6.7.
  • Scripted sweeps compute these without participants.
  • They describe cursor motion, not what people perceive.
Notes

This is the parameter exploration in the evaluation: sweeps over a grid of k, gamma and n, shown as small multiples. Which settings people perceive best is a question for the later study.

3 · Technical approach · 2D to 3D

From screen to print

  • Try an encoding on screen
  • Print the same encoding as a 3D model
  • Compare the two in a later study
Notes

For surface data such as terrain and heatmaps, the value-to-height mapping reuses the same quantization and shape as the gain, so the printed model and the on-screen version encode the data the same way. The pipeline exports an STL file any 3D printer can use. Comparing the two with participants is future work; this term the pipeline is the deliverable. It answers the future-work question of whether encodings chosen on screen still work once printed.

3 · Technical approach · screen versus print

Closing the gap: screen versus print

Gap may close

  • Finding extremes and hot spots
  • Comparing two magnitudes
  • Following a path downhill
  • Sensing sharp boundaries

Screen only

  • Change the mapping instantly
  • Hide the picture to test touch alone
  • Time-varying data
  • Replay and logs for studies
  • Runs in any browser

Print only

  • Real shape under the fingers
  • Two hands, many fingers
  • Works without vision
  • An object to hold and share
  • The question: does the mapping that works best on screen also work best in print?

Hypotheses for the later study, not results

Notes

The arc: map on screen, recreate in print, compare. The channels differ, friction on screen and height in print, so the same mapping means the same function: height reuses q and gamma, with relief height playing the role of k. Where the rankings of candidate mappings agree, the screen is a valid place to choose encodings; where they disagree, we have found the gap. This term produces matched pairs, a mapping on screen plus its STL; the comparison itself needs participants.

5 · Evaluation

Evaluating without participants

  • Mapping exploration: which data-to-touch mappings the tool can express, and what each does to the cursor
  • Candidate mappings: a short list per task, chosen for the later study
  • Rendering fidelity: gain matches the data; replay is exact
  • Usage scenarios: terrain tasks, walked through by me

Toolkit evaluation strategies, Ledo et al. 2018 · terrain tasks, Herman et al. 2024

Notes

The central question is which tactile mappings work: which felt channel, with which settings, carries which data for which task. This term maps that design space without participants. Each mapping is a channel plus its parameters k, gamma and n; scripted sweeps show what it does to the cursor, as traversal profiles in small multiples. From these I pick a few candidate mappings per task, which become the conditions of the later study. Fidelity uses scripted cursor sweeps, and replay must rebuild a session exactly; this checks the rendering, not perception. The usage scenarios are tracing a path downhill, comparing two heights and finding the extremes on Maunga Whau. No participants this term, so no human-subjects review is needed.

6 · Limitations and future work

Limitations and future work

  • Friction alone may not be enough to convey data accurately enough to guide encoding decisions.
  • Input hardware is everywhere but not uniform. Mice, trackpads and pointer acceleration all change cursor movement, the quantity pseudo-haptics manipulates.
  • Only some properties may work through a mouse. Early pseudo-haptic stiffness used an isometric input device (Lécuyer et al. 2000).
  • Parameter exploration measures cursor motion, not perception. Perceived friction may not scale linearly with gain.
  • The sensations are illusory and depend on vision. The tool informs what to fabricate; it does not replace fabrication or serve non-visual readers.

Future work

  • Which felt differences can people tell apart through a cursor?
  • Does touch add information when vision is busy with another variable?
  • Do encodings chosen on screen still work once printed?
Notes

Same limitations and open questions as Section 6 of the written proposal. Keep it brief; the future-work questions are the launchpad.

Likely question

Why feel what you can already see?

  • The final product may be physical. A print or a shape display is explored by touch, so its encoding has to work by touch. The screen is where to try it first.
  • Touch may carry a second variable. Colour can show one value while stickiness carries another. Whether that helps analysis is an open question.
  • The tool makes the test cheap. Hide the picture and check whether the feel alone still carries the pattern.
  • Not real touch, and not an accessibility tool. Pseudo-haptics needs vision; it is a stand-in while designing.
Notes

Beginners in haptics will most likely ask this first. Keep the answer to the first bullet unless they push.

Theory

Why do illusions happen?

  • The image on the retina is ambiguous. Many different worlds could produce the same image.
  • Bottom-up. Some illusions come from the wiring of the eye itself, such as simultaneous contrast and café wall.
  • Top-down. Most come from expectation. The brain picks the most likely cause of the image (Helmholtz, Gregory).
  • Depth cues make the brain enlarge what looks farther away, which explains Müller-Lyer, Ponzo and the vanishing point (Gregory 1968).
  • Bayesian perception puts both together. What we see is the signal weighted by a prior, the brain’s expectation of what is likely (Knill & Pouget 2004).

Helmholtz 1867 · Gregory 1968, 1980 · Knill & Pouget 2004

Notes

Contrast and café wall are the eye. The size illusions are the brain applying a rule that is usually right. Bayesian perception is the modern way to say this, and the next slide uses the same weighting for when two senses disagree.

Theory

Separate senses, one percept

Each sense is studied in its own field, yet one sense can change what another reports.

  • McGurk effect: seeing lips say “ga” while hearing “ba” makes people hear “da”.
  • Ventriloquism: a voice seems to come from the puppet’s moving mouth.
  • Rubber hand: watching a rubber hand being stroked makes people feel it as their own.
  • Visual capture: through a lens that distorts a square, people feel the shape they see.

McGurk & MacDonald 1976 · Alais & Burr 2004 · Botvinick & Cohen 1998 · Rock & Victor 1964

Notes

Vision, hearing and touch each have their own receptors, brain areas and research communities. None of these effects makes sense with one sense alone.

Theory

What visualization assumed

Mostly bottom-up

  • Bertin’s visual variables and Cleveland & McGill’s graphical perception measure how accurately people decode a visual signal.
  • Preattentive features decide which encodings pop out (Healey & Enns 2012).

Top-down gets in

  • What people already know changes what they see in a chart (Xiong et al. 2020).
  • People compare charts through shortcuts such as average length, and these can be fooled on purpose (Jardine et al. 2020, Ondov et al. 2020).
  • Pseudo-haptics uses both. A bottom-up conflict between hand and eye is resolved top-down into a sense of touch.

Bertin 1967 · Cleveland & McGill 1984 · Healey & Enns 2012 · Xiong et al. 2020 · Jardine et al. 2020 · Ondov et al. 2020

Motivation

An apparent paradox

  • The design goal: lower cognitive load. Visualization moves work from thinking to seeing.
  • The capability: load vision more. Pseudo-haptics adds touch by putting more work on vision.
  • Separate senses usually draw on separate resources, which is why touch normally helps (Wickens 2008). Pseudo-haptic touch arrives through vision, so it may not get that benefit.
  • But perceptual load is not cognitive load (Lavie et al. 2004). Extra seeing does not have to mean extra thinking.
  • Everyday example: some viewers choose subtitles over a dub, taking on more visual work to keep the original.
Notes

It only looks like a paradox from visualization’s side. Whether the extra load is perceptual and cheap, or cognitive and costly, is a question this tool can study.
