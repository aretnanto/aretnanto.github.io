Feel Data on Screen

A Pseudo-Haptic Prototyping Tool for Vis

Aditya Retnanto · November 4, 2026

Notes

Pseudo-haptics creates a feeling of touch with an ordinary mouse and screen. I want to use it to prototype touch for visualization.

Motivation

Visual analytics runs on vision

Visualization is “the use of computer-supported, interactive, visual representations of data to amplify cognition.”
Card, Mackinlay & Shneiderman 1999

  • Interaction is a loop of input and output, and on a desktop the output is visual. The result of every action comes back through the eyes.
  • Direct manipulation lets people act on the data itself, for example brushing points or dragging a node, and see the result right away (Shneiderman 1983). It borrows the metaphor of handling an object directly, but the hand never touches the data. It only moves a mouse.
  • The mouse is one-way. It tells the computer where the hand went, and tells the hand nothing about the data. Touch, the sense direct manipulation is modeled on, is left out of the loop.
Notes

The definition keeps the word visual. Input comes from the hand, but output only goes to the eyes. Direct manipulation borrows the language of touch, and the peripheral is what keeps touch out.

Theory

Why do illusions happen?

  • The image on the retina is ambiguous. Many different worlds could produce the same image.
  • Bottom-up. Some illusions come from the wiring of the eye itself, such as simultaneous contrast and café wall.
  • Top-down. Most come from expectation. The brain picks the most likely cause of the image (Helmholtz, Gregory).
  • Depth cues make the brain enlarge what looks farther away, which explains Müller-Lyer, Ponzo and the vanishing point (Gregory 1968).
  • Bayesian perception puts both together. What we see is the signal weighted by a prior, the brain’s expectation of what is likely (Knill & Pouget 2004).

Helmholtz 1867 · Gregory 1968, 1980 · Knill & Pouget 2004

Notes

Go back to the grid. Contrast and café wall are the eye. The size illusions are the brain applying a rule that is usually right. Bayesian perception is the modern way to say this, and the next slide uses the same weighting for when two senses disagree.

Theory

Separate senses, one percept

Each sense is studied in its own field, yet one sense can change what another reports.

McGurk effect

Seeing lips say “ga” while hearing “ba” makes people hear “da”.

Ventriloquism

A voice seems to come from the puppet’s moving mouth.

Rubber hand

Watching a rubber hand being stroked makes people feel it as their own.

Visual capture

Through a lens that distorts a square, people feel the shape they see.

McGurk & MacDonald 1976 · Alais & Burr 2004 · Botvinick & Cohen 1998 · Rock & Victor 1964

Notes

Vision, hearing and touch each have their own receptors, brain areas and research communities. None of these effects makes sense with one sense alone.

Theory

When senses conflict, vision usually wins

  • The brain weights each sense by how reliable it is (Ernst & Banks 2002).
  • Vision is usually the most precise, so it usually wins.

Ernst & Banks 2002

Notes

Vision is sharp and touch is broad, so what you perceive lands close to what you see. This is the Bayesian weighting from the illusions slide, applied across senses.

Motivation

Can we design multisensory illusions?

If vision usually wins a conflict, the visual signal can be designed on purpose to change what another sense perceives.

Pseudo-haptics

Slowing the cursor makes people feel friction and resistance through a plain mouse.

Lécuyer et al. 2000

Notes

Pause on the question, then reveal the answer. So far illusions were things that happen to us. Here one becomes a design material.

Background

Pseudo-haptics

  • Lécuyer and colleagues changed how far the cursor moves for the same hand movement, the control/display ratio.
  • When the cursor slows down, people feel friction or resistance, with no force feedback device (Lécuyer et al. 2000).
  • With only a mouse, people felt bumps and holes on a flat image (Lécuyer et al. 2004).

It needs only a mouse and a screen.

Motivation

If we can engineer touch, what can it carry?

Channels to encode

  • Friction, where values feel sticky
  • Weight, where heavier items move slower
  • Stiffness, how hard something pushes back
  • Roughness, small catches along a path
  • Snapping, a pull toward a target
  • Slope, easier downhill than uphill

Applications to build

  • Finding a pattern in a heatmap
  • Selecting a community in a network
  • Reading the peaks of a terrain
  • Reshaping data and replaying the history

Answering this needs a way to try encodings quickly, which motivates a prototyping tool.

Motivation

Touch costs time and hardware

Almost everyone has a computer and a mouse. Few people have a printer.

Jansen et al. 2015 · Follmer et al. 2013 · Bae et al. 2022, 2023 · Mueller et al. 2014 · Lécuyer 2009

Notes

Physicalizations give real touch, and Bae et al. showed prints can even sense touch, but only when sensing is designed into the print. A print takes minutes to overnight, so designers iterate about once a day. Pseudo-haptics is the fast, low-fidelity end, and the cursor already knows where the hand is.

The project

A pseudo-haptic prototyping tool for vis

A browser tool where designers map data to a feel, test whether it comes across without vision, and decide what is worth fabricating.

Understand

What touch can carry

Measure which felt differences people tell apart, and turn them into design requirements such as tactile palettes, before anything is printed.

Realize

A new display medium

The cursor itself becomes a way to display data through touch on a common computer.

Users

  • Visualization and physicalization designers and researchers

Analytical tasks

  • Find a pattern by feel
  • Separate significant structure from noise
  • Reshape data and review how it changed
  • Compare the same data on screen and in print

Theory

What visualization assumed

Mostly bottom-up

  • Bertin’s visual variables and Cleveland & McGill’s graphical perception measure how accurately people decode a visual signal.
  • Preattentive features decide which encodings pop out (Healey & Enns 2012).

Top-down gets in

  • What people already know changes what they see in a chart (Xiong et al. 2020).
  • People compare charts through shortcuts such as average length, and these can be fooled on purpose (Jardine et al. 2020, Ondov et al. 2020).

Pseudo-haptics uses both. A bottom-up conflict between hand and eye is resolved top-down into a sense of touch.

Bertin 1967 · Cleveland & McGill 1984 · Healey & Enns 2012 · Xiong et al. 2020 · Jardine et al. 2020 · Ondov et al. 2020

Motivation

An apparent paradox

The design goal

Lower cognitive load

Visualization moves work from thinking to seeing.

The capability

Load vision more

Pseudo-haptics adds a touch channel by putting more work on vision, the sense we usually add touch to relieve.

  • Separate senses usually draw on separate resources, which is why touch normally helps (Wickens 2008).
  • Pseudo-haptic touch arrives through vision, so it may not get that benefit.
  • But perceptual load is not cognitive load (Lavie et al. 2004). Extra seeing does not have to mean extra thinking.
Everyday example

Subtitles

Many viewers prefer reading subtitles to hearing a dub. They take on more visual work to avoid a mismatch between lips and voice, and to keep the original.

Notes

It only looks like a paradox from visualization’s side. Whether the extra load is perceptual and cheap, or cognitive and costly, is a question this tool can study.
